# transcribed-transformer

Research towards models that answer compliance questions over collections of ASR call transcripts, and a measurement of how much architecture and pretraining distribution matter for that task.

> **Status (2026-10-05): the re-run queue under the fixed loop is complete (seventeen runs, record linked below); every number in this table is from a sound run or marked as from an abandoned one.** A gradient-synchronisation bug in the two-GPU data-parallel training loop (found 2026-09-28; every affected run trained on half the data at half the batch) invalidated the absolute numbers of most arms. Seventeen runs completed under the fixed loop between 2026-09-28 and 2026-10-05, thirteen re-runs of abandoned arms and four new arms (the 270M-decoder column) (the record is linked from the E1 row); the Qwen3 E1 and E2 ladder, the two E6 hybrids and the E5+ pair were not re-run and their rows say so. After the re-runs, under both golds: the native encoder-decoders lead the decoders of their own size on zero-shot transfer and on evidence, the sound 3 to 4B decoders are level with the 2B native pair rather than ahead of it, and an encoder stitched in front of a small decoder is worth most where the decoder can do least on its own.

## TLDR — context and motivation

Compliance question answering over call transcripts is a task where reported results and deployed results tend to diverge. Models that look strong on benchmarks built from clean written text do noticeably worse once the input is genuine ASR output, and the usual response is to reach for a frontier model behind an API. That works. It is also an expensive answer to a task that ought to be tractable at a fraction of the size.

The wager here is that the cause is the input itself. **Transcribed speech is disfluent, and it is long.** Fillers, restarts, repairs and recognition errors are not noise to be cleaned away before the real work starts; they *are* the distribution. And a compliance judgement usually depends on how the whole conversation fits together, while causal attention builds each part of the transcript's representation without seeing what comes after it.

This repository exists to put numbers on that rather than assert it, and to train models that work on ASR-mangled text instead of in spite of it.

## The problem

In regulated industries, conversations between staff and customers are recorded, transcribed, and reviewed against compliance questions. The questions vary in kind and are usually written by the reviewing organisation: *did the advisor communicate clearly*, *did the customer show signs of vulnerability*, *did the customer make a complaint*, *was it handled properly*.

The task has a specific and unusual shape:

- **Input is long, but not uniformly.** A single transcript can run to tens of thousands of tokens, while a great many are far shorter. A *case* is the whole interaction with one customer: several transcripts, judged together.
- **The text is diarised ASR output.** Speaker-labelled turns, one turn per line (rendered to the model as `<line number>: <speaker>: <text>`, so evidence can be cited by line), with disfluencies, recognition errors, and machine-restored punctuation. Roles are not labelled: who is the advisor and who is the customer has to be inferred from the conversation, and calls are not reliably two-party — spouses, relatives and colleagues join advice calls, which regulatory guidance on vulnerable customers explicitly anticipates.
- **Output is short and structured.** For each question: one answer, the set of transcript line numbers that constitute evidence, and a short justification.
- **Questions are open-ended, and so are their answers.** New questions are written all the time, by different organisations. The model receives the question text, the permitted answers, and the grading rule for each one, all as input. It cannot rely on a fixed, trained-in label set: the same underlying judgement is variously expressed as `{yes, no}`, as `{pass, partial pass, fail, NA}`, or as opaque codes. A question never seen during training must work. *(Scope today: the question bank and the data schema use one four-value vocabulary, `pass | fail | partial_pass | NA`, with each question choosing its subset; other vocabularies are the design intent and not yet supported by the schema.)*
- **Case semantics differ per question.** Some questions pass if *any* call satisfies them; others only if *every* call does. The model has to infer which from the question.

Scope: **speech transcripts only.** Chat logs, scanned documents and emails are excluded, because the hypotheses below are specifically about ASR distribution and disfluency.

## Two hypotheses

**1. The architecture is mismatched.** Long input, short structured output, and judgements that depend on the conversation as a whole. Under causal attention the answer is generated after the whole transcript and can attend to all of it, but every transcript token is encoded without seeing anything later: the opening of a call is represented without knowing how the call ends. A question like "was this complaint handled properly" depends on how early and late turns relate, so bidirectional attention, where every transcript token is encoded in light of the whole call, should be the better inductive bias.

**2. The pretraining distribution is mismatched.** Models are pretrained on written text. The input here is disfluent transcribed speech, where fillers, repairs and repetition carry real signal, and recognition errors are systematic rather than random. Pretraining on transcript-like text should help.

## Design — the experiments

Six trained designs were considered. (A frontier API baseline, E0, was planned and withdrawn on 2026-09-29: the benchmark's gold labels came partly from the same model family, so no fair comparison was possible; its record stays under `experiments/` as history.) The original arms come from one model family, Qwen3, so that an arm differs from the control in exactly one thing: the attention mask, or the architecture built from the same weights. The native encoder-decoders (E4), the hybrids (E6) and the stitched designs (E4-mix-and-match, E5) come from the Gemma 3 and T5Gemma 2 family, each with a decoder-only control from the same family under the same recipe, and three cross-family decoders serve as matched controls. Every arm runs at about 1.7B first (full fine-tuning fits one GPU), then larger sizes for the arms that survive; same data, same split, same `<n>: <role>: <text>` rendering, loss on the answer only with zero prompt loss, a 32k sequence length for the Qwen arms and a 16k cap for the Gemma-family arms. Verdicts come from a literature review (2026-09-10); results are `TBD` until a run produces them and are recorded per run in `experiments/`.

**What the benchmark can and cannot say (2026-09-29, D61).** Every result is reported under two golds and nothing else. **Gold = teacher** is the headline: the label the training labeller itself produced (qwen3.8 for general_qa, Sonnet for the vulnerability, complaint and end-of-discussion families), on all 17,520 clean and 20,700 messy benchmark pairs. It measures how well an arm learned its teacher, which is what the two hypotheses need (the arms are comparable with each other), and it is not absolute accuracy: predicting each question's majority answer without reading the transcript scores macro-F1 0.569 clean and 0.563 messy. **Gold = opus** is Opus's own label on the 4,012 pairs it labelled as a second, independent labeller (3,410 clean): the general_qa pairs where the teacher and a second local labeller disagreed and the family pairs the teacher called `fail`, a selected, hard subset whose numbers compare arms with each other and never with the teacher numbers (its majority floors on the clean pairs are 0.709 on Opus's own majority answer and 0.354 on the teacher's; 0.712 and 0.352 messy). Zero-shot transfer is read from the held-out questions without a twin in training (`cell=unseen_q_no_twin`, 2,160 clean pairs, majority floor 0.582; twenty of the 51 held-out questions are rewordings of trained ones, so the full held-out cell reads optimistic). No model that did not train on these labels can be compared fairly on either gold, which is why the API baseline was withdrawn. Numbers in this README are gold = teacher clean macro-F1 / no-twin unseen-question macro-F1, followed by the same under gold = opus; every results table also carries evidence F1, format validity and the messy variant. See `data/DATASHEET.md`, "Known limitations of the labelled data".

Every arm is scored on the same benchmark: cases built from public call corpora, questions with explicit answer options and grading rules, labels produced by LLM labelling of real, unmodified calls (a labeller answers each question with evidence first; a separate LLM-as-a-judge step assesses label quality), each call in a clean and a messy variant where the corpus has both (17,520 of the 20,700 benchmark pairs: 11,000 AppTek pairs whose messy side is the corpus's real ASR, and 6,520 Taskmaster and ACI-Bench pairs whose messy side is generated by the ASR channel model; the podcast corpus is real-ASR only), so the cost of transcription noise is measured directly on shared pairs. The generator and its data format are documented in [`src/synthesis/README.md`](src/synthesis/README.md) and the DATASHEET's "Labelled data" section.

Results are recorded, never estimated; negative results get written up the same as positive ones.
